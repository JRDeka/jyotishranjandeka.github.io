Research

Protected areas are only part of the landscape.

My research asks what happens outside their boundaries: where tigers can move, how connectivity changes their interactions with people, and how populations remain linked across political borders.

Northeast India is my primary field landscape, but the questions extend across the wider tiger range.

01

Tiger connectivity

Where can tigers still move?

I identify corridors that connect tiger populations and examine how forest structure, prey, infrastructure, settlements, and other human pressures shape those connections.

02

People + wildlife

When does connectivity become coexistence—or conflict?

I study how connected wildlife landscapes overlap with human settlement and land use, and whether connectivity helps explain where interactions occur and how severe they become.

03

Transboundary conservation

What happens when ecological landscapes cross political borders?

I examine how tiger populations and corridors connect across international boundaries, and which links matter most for long-term persistence and coordinated conservation.

Current work

Four questions shaping my PhD.

Different datasets and methods, but one connected problem: how large mammals persist in rapidly changing landscapes.

Connectivity

Northeast India

Tiger corridors beyond protected areas

Mapping where tiger movement is still possible and identifying the landscape features that maintain—or erode—connections among populations.

Coexistence

India

Connectivity and human–wildlife interactions

Testing whether wildlife connectivity helps explain the occurrence and severity of interactions with tigers, leopards, elephants, and wild boar.

Transboundary

South & Southeast Asia

Tiger populations across borders

Evaluating cross-border corridors, prey-supported movement landscapes, and the implications of barriers and landscape change for long-term persistence.

Acoustic monitoring

Orang National Park

Can we identify individual tigers by their voices?

Developing non-invasive acoustic and machine-learning approaches to detect tiger vocalizations and test whether calls contain reliable individual signatures.

About

I work at the intersection of field ecology and landscape science.

I am a PhD candidate at the University of Wisconsin–Madison studying Bengal tigers and other large mammals, with a particular focus on connected landscapes in Northeast India.

My broader goal is to produce science that helps conservation move beyond isolated protected areas toward landscapes where wildlife populations can remain connected while people continue to live and work.
