Research

Connected landscapes are at the center of my work.

Protected areas are essential, but wildlife does not stop at their boundaries. I study what happens between them: movement, coexistence, ecological connectivity, and the technologies that can help us monitor wildlife across large landscapes.

01

Tiger Connectivity

How do tigers move between protected areas?

I study where tiger populations remain connected, what limits movement, and which corridors are most important for long-term persistence.

Corridor ecology · Landscape connectivity · Tiger conservation

02

Human–Wildlife Coexistence

Why do people and large mammals come into conflict in some landscapes but not others?

I examine how wildlife connectivity, protected areas, human development, and the wildland–urban interface shape human–wildlife interactions.

Human–wildlife interactions · WUI · Connectivity

03

Transboundary Conservation

How can wildlife remain connected across international borders?

I study transboundary tiger connectivity to identify important movement routes, vulnerable corridors, and opportunities for coordinated conservation.

Transboundary ecology · Corridors · Metapopulations

04

Conservation Technology

Can we monitor tigers in new ways?

I use camera traps, passive acoustics, remote sensing, and machine learning to develop non-invasive approaches for monitoring tigers and other large mammals.

Passive acoustics · Camera traps · AI · Remote sensing

Current work

About

Science for connected landscapes.

My broader goal is to understand how large mammals can persist in increasingly fragmented and human-dominated landscapes—and to translate ecological evidence into practical conservation strategies.

I am a PhD candidate in the Department of Forest & Wildlife Ecology at the University of Wisconsin–Madison. My work focuses especially on Bengal tigers and other large mammals in Northeast India.
